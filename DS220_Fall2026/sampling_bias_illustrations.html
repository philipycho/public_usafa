Simulate a world where we know the truth

We hold a census of the whole Cadet Wing — all 4035 cadets — in simulated_cadets.csv. Because the data are synthetic, we can compute any population parameter exactly. In real life we never can; we only see a sample.

  • (IMS §2.1.1) Census — collecting data on every case in the entire population.
  • (IMS §2.1.2) Parameter — a numerical summary of the whole population (fixed, usually unknown). Statistic — the same summary computed from a sample (known, changes sample to sample).

These numbers are fixed and known. Each slide that follows tries to recover one of them from a sample — sometimes well, sometimes not.

Simple random sample — the gold standard

The process. Give every cadet an equal chance and keep 400. A sound method — our yardstick for “unbiased.”

Statistic of interest: the wing’s mean GPA.

observed <- cadets %>% slice_sample(n = 400)     # each cadet equally likely
Quantity Parameter (truth) Statistic (our sample) Bias (stat - truth)
mean GPA 2.683 2.708 0.025

Method: simple random sample — bias ≈ 0. (IMS §2.1.4) A simple random sample gives every case an equal chance of selection, and knowing one case is in the sample tells you nothing about which others are — like drawing names from a hat. No trait controls who gets in, so the statistic lands on the parameter.

Stratified random sample — split, then sample each group

The process. Divide the wing into strata by class year, then take a simple random sample within each (10% of every class). Guarantees every class is represented in proportion.

Statistic of interest: the wing’s mean GPA.

observed <- cadets %>%
  group_by(grad_year) %>%         # strata = class years
  slice_sample(prop = 0.10) %>%   # SRS within each stratum
  ungroup()
Quantity Parameter (truth) Statistic (our sample) Bias (stat - truth)
mean GPA 2.683 2.653 -0.03

Method: stratified random sample — bias ≈ 0. (IMS §2.1.5) Stratified sampling divides the population into strata of similar cases, then draws a simple random sample within each stratum. Because every class year is represented in proportion, the estimate is both unbiased and typically more precise than a plain SRS.

Cluster sample — sample whole groups

The process. Treat the 40 squadrons as clusters; randomly pick 4 and survey everyone in them. Cheaper to run (four hallways, not the whole wing).

Statistic of interest: the wing’s mean GPA.

chosen   <- sample(1:40, size = 4)               # 4 random squadrons
observed <- cadets %>% filter(squadron %in% chosen)
Quantity Parameter (truth) Statistic (our sample) Bias (stat - truth)
mean GPA 2.683 2.634 -0.049

Method: cluster sample — bias small here. (IMS §2.1.5) A cluster sample breaks the population into clusters, randomly selects a fixed number of clusters, and includes every case in those chosen. It works when clusters each resemble the whole — as our squadrons do (cadets are assigned across them). If clusters differed systematically, cluster sampling could mislead.

Convenience sample → selection bias

The process. You survey whoever is easy to reach — cadets in the library at 2200. The heavier a cadet’s study habit, the likelier they’re there, so avg_study_hours itself drives who gets in.

Statistic of interest: the wing’s mean weekly study hours.

# selection probability climbs with study hours (library-goers)
observed <- cadets %>% slice_sample(n = 400, weight_by = avg_study_hours^3)
Quantity Parameter (truth) Statistic (our sample) Bias (stat - truth)
mean study hrs/wk 17.285 19.773 2.488

Issue: selection bias (a convenience sample) — estimate too high. (IMS §2.1.4) A convenience sample is one where the individuals who are easiest to reach are more likely to be included, and it is often unclear what sub-population it actually represents. The library over-represents heavy studiers.

Undercoverage / selection bias → a whole group can’t be seen

The process. You email the survey through academic-major department listservs. Cadets with no declared major — mostly freshmen — are on no list, so their chance of selection is zero.

Statistic of interest: the freshman share of the wing.

observed <- cadets %>% filter(academic_major != "Undeclared")
Quantity Parameter (truth) Statistic (our sample) Bias (stat - truth)
freshman share of wing 0.275 0.115 -0.159

Issue: undercoverage / selection bias — freshmen nearly vanish (≈28% → ≈12%). (from NTI) Undercoverage / selection bias occurs when the sampling frame systematically leaves out part of the population, giving those cases little or no chance of selection. Any statistic tied to class year is now bent, and a bigger sample from the same list would not help.

Voluntary response sample → the opinionated opt in

The process. You post an optional “Cadet Fitness Feedback” link. Cadets proud of their fitness (high pea, athletes) are far likelier to click.

Statistic of interest: the wing’s mean PEA (fitness score).

# opt-in probability climbs steeply with fitness
observed <- cadets %>% slice_sample(n = 600, weight_by = pea^4)
Quantity Parameter (truth) Statistic (our sample) Bias (stat - truth)
mean PEA 2.835 3.317 0.482

Issue: voluntary response bias — estimate too high. (from NTI) In a voluntary response sample, people self-select by opting in, so those who feel strongly about the topic are over-represented. Here volunteers select on the very trait being measured (fitness).

Non-response bias → a fair draw, but some won’t answer

The process. Selection is fair — a proper SRS of 800. But it is an academic survey, and stronger students are likelier to complete it. The damage is in who answers, not who was chosen.

Statistic of interest: the wing’s mean GPA.

frame    <- cadets %>% slice_sample(n = 800)                # fair selection
observed <- frame  %>%                                       # ...but who replies?
  filter(rbinom(n(), 1, plogis(1.6 * (gpa - mu_gpa))) == 1)
Quantity Parameter (truth) Statistic (our sample) Bias (stat - truth)
mean GPA 2.683 3.002 0.32

Issue: non-response bias — estimate too high. (IMS §2.1.4) Even when people are picked at random, if the non-response rate is high it is unclear whether results represent the population; this non-response bias can skew results. It takes both halves — many go silent and the silent ones differ (lower GPA). Contrast undercoverage: there the low-GPA group was never selectable; here they were selected and slipped away.

Response bias → they answer, but the measurement lies

The process. Nobody is dropped — an SRS where everyone replies. But “How many hours do you really study?” invites cadets to round up to look diligent. The number is bent at the moment it is recorded.

Statistic of interest: the wing’s mean reported study hours.

observed <- cadets %>%
  slice_sample(n = 400) %>%
  mutate(reported_hours = avg_study_hours + rnorm(n(), mean = 4, sd = 1.5))
Quantity Parameter (truth) Statistic (our sample) Bias (stat - truth)
mean reported study hrs 17.285 21.301 4.015

Issue: response bias — estimate too high, though the sample was representative. (from NTI) Response bias arises when respondents do answer, but the measurement process — question wording, who is asking, a sensitive topic, or the wish to look good — skews the answers. More cadets or better sampling can’t fix a biased instrument; only a better question can.

Confounding variable → an association that isn’t cause

Not a sampling flaw — a study-design trap. Even with the full census (no sampling at all), a naive comparison can still mislead us about cause.

The claim / question: “Fitter cadets study less — does fitness steal study time?” Compare mean study hours for high- vs. low-PEA cadets.

What we actually built in (the SCM truth). This data is synthetic, so we know the real causal arrows behind it:

  • is_ic → pea (+) — varsity athletes are fitter (higher PEA).
  • is_ic → avg_study_hours (−) — athletes have less free time, so they study fewer hours.
  • There is no arrow pea → avg_study_hours — fitness has zero causal effect on how much a cadet studies.

So any fitness–studying association in the data has to be manufactured by the common cause is_ic, not by fitness itself.

overall <- cadets %>%
  mutate(fitness = if_else(pea >= median(pea), "high PEA", "low PEA")) %>%
  group_by(fitness) %>%
  summarize(mean_study = round(mean(avg_study_hours), 2), .groups = "drop")
fitness mean_study
high PEA 16.71
low PEA 17.87

What the data suggests: high-PEA cadets study about 1.2 hours less — exactly the “fitness steals study time” story. But we know that arrow does not exist, so this must be the confounder talking.

Hold the confounder fixed. Split the same comparison by athlete status (is_ic):

group fitness mean_study
IC athlete high PEA 14.78
IC athlete low PEA 15.26
non-athlete high PEA 18.67
non-athlete low PEA 18.02

Issue: is_ic is a confounding variable. Inside each athlete group the gap collapses — and even flips (among non-athletes, high-PEA cadets study slightly more). This matches the SCM exactly: with no pea → study arrow, the negative link was never causal — it was is_ic pushing pea up and study hours down at the same time. (The small positive residual among non-athletes is a second, weaker common cause: seniors are both a bit fitter and study a bit more.)

Truth vs. data, side by side: the SCM says the causal effect of fitness on studying is 0; the raw data suggested −1.2 hours; holding is_ic fixed sends it back toward 0. That gap between “what the arrows say” and “what the averages say” is confounding. (IMS §2.2.1) A confounding variable is associated with both the explanatory and response variables; because it is tied to both, it prevents concluding that the explanatory variable caused the response — the cadet version of ice-cream sales & boating accidents (both driven by summer heat).

The big picture

How the data were collected Effect on the statistic
simple random / stratified / cluster (sound) lands on the parameter (trustworthy)
convenience sample (reachable group) bent toward whoever’s easy to reach
undercoverage / selection (frame misses a group) that group’s traits vanish from the estimate
voluntary response (opt-in) bent toward the opinionated / the measured trait
non-response (fair draw, silent subgroup) bent toward those who bother to answer
response bias (measurement) bent by the question, even with a perfect sample
confounding (common cause) association ≠ cause
  • A biased collection method is not cured by a bigger sample.
  • Always ask first: “How were these observations collected?” ```
