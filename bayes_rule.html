Understanding Bayes’ Rule

Motivation

Bayes’ Rule gives us a formal, mathematically rigorous way to update our beliefs about something uncertain (like “does a patient have cancer?”) after observing new evidence (like “positive test result”). Bayes’ Rule tells us how to combine what we already believed (our prior) with what we just observed (the likelihood of that observation) to get an updated belief (the posterior).

This kind of reasoning shows up in many applications:

  • Medicine: How likely is it that a patient has a disease, given that they tested positive?
  • Spam filters: How likely is an email to be spam, given that it contains the word “free”?
  • Law: How likely is a defendant to be guilty, given a piece of evidence?
  • Sports: How likely is a rookie actually an all-star player, given they play well in their first 5 games?

In every one of these cases, we want to reverse the direction of a conditional probability: we know (or can estimate) \(P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{hypothesis})\), but what we care about is \(P(\text{hypothesis} \mid \text{evidence})\). Bayes’ Rule is the tool that lets us flip the conditioning around.

Mathematical Definition

For two events \(A\) and \(B\) with \(P(B) > 0\):

\[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A) \, P(A)}{P(B)} \]

Each term has a name:

  • \(P(A \mid B)\) — the posterior: our updated belief about \(A\) after seeing evidence \(B\).
  • \(P(B \mid A)\) — the likelihood: how probable the evidence \(B\) is, if \(A\) were true.
  • \(P(A)\) — the prior: our belief about \(A\) before seeing any evidence.
  • \(P(B)\) — the marginal probability of the evidence (sometimes called the “normalizing constant”): how probable \(B\) is overall, across all possibilities of \(A\).

When \(A\) has only two possible outcomes (\(A\) and its complement, \(A^c\)), the denominator can be expanded using the law of total probability:

\[ \begin{align*} P(B) &= P(B \cap A) + P(B \cap A^c) \\[6pt] &= P(B \mid A)\,P(A) + P(B \mid A^c)\,P(A^c) \end{align*} \]

which gives the form of Bayes’ Rule that we will use below:

\[ P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B \mid A)\,P(A) + P(B \mid A^c)\,P(A^c)} \]

More generally, suppose \(A\) can take on any of \(n\) mutually exclusive and exhaustive values \(A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n\) (a partition of the sample space — every outcome falls into exactly one of these categories). The law of total probability then expands the denominator as a sum over all the possibilities, not just two:

\[ P(B) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(B \mid A_i)\,P(A_i) \]

which gives the general form of Bayes’ Rule:

\[ P(A_j \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A_j)\,P(A_j)}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n} P(B \mid A_i)\,P(A_i)} \]

Here, the numerator is the joint probability of the specific outcome \(A_j\) occurring together with the evidence \(B\) (i.e., \(P(A_j \cap B)\)), and the denominator sums these joint probabilities across every possible outcome of \(A\) (\(A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n\)).

The quantity \(P(A_j \mid B)\) is our posterior belief about one specific value \(A_j\), given the evidence \(B\). It answers the question: “given that we observed \(B\), how likely is it that \(A\) took on the specific value \(A_j\)?”

Because this formula works for any choice of \(j\), we can generate an updated belief or posterior for each possible value, \(A_j\), by changing the numerator.

Cancer Example

What we are trying to solve for

A patient takes a diagnostic test for a certain type of cancer, and the test comes back positive. The patient wants to know: What is the probability that I actually have cancer, given that I tested positive?.

In notation, if \(C\) denotes “has cancer” and \(T^+\) denotes “tested positive,” we are looking for:

\[ P(C \mid T^+) \]

Before the positive test result, the probability that the patient has cancer would be \(P(C)\). But after we observe a positive test result, we have to update our belief about the probability of having cancer given the new evidence.

What we assume is known

Diagnostic tests are validated using two key properties, and we are told the prevalence of cancer in the population:

Quantity Symbol Value
Sensitivity: probability of testing positive given you have cancer \(P(T^+ \mid C)\) 0.85
Specificity: probability of testing negative given you don’t have cancer \(P(T^- \mid C^c)\) 0.95
Prevalence: probability a random person has cancer \(P(C)\) 0.01

From these, we can derive everything else we need:

  • \(P(C^c) = 1 - P(C) = 1 - 0.01 = 0.99\) (99% of the population is cancer-free)
  • \(P(T^+ \mid C^c) = 1 - P(T^- \mid C^c) = 1 - 0.95 = 0.05\) (false positive rate)
  • \(P(T^- \mid C) = 1 - P(T^+ \mid C) = 1 - 0.85 = 0.15\) (false negative rate)

Notice that the known conditional probabilities, such as \(P(T^+ \mid C)\), provide the probability of a specific test result given we know whether cancer exists or not. We are interested in solving for the opposite: what is the probability a patient has cancer given a test result.

Visualizing with a probability tree

The tree diagram below helps visualize the sequence of events. The first split shows a patient either has cancer or not with probability \(P(C)\) and \(P(C^c)\), respectively. The second split shows whether a patient tests positive or negative given that they have cancer or not. Multiplying probabilities along a branch gives the joint probability of that path (recall the general multiplication rule \(P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B)\)).

Applying Bayes’ Rule

We want \(P(C \mid T^+)\). By Bayes’ Rule:

\[ P(C \mid T^+) = \frac{P(T^+ \mid C)\,P(C)}{P(T^+)} \]

The denominator, \(P(T^+)\), is the marginal probability of testing positive, obtained by summing the joint probabilities of every path in the tree that ends in “Test +” (law of total probability). Importantly, we have to account for the possibility that a patient tests positive even though they are cancer-free, \(P(T^+ \mid C^c)\).

\[ \begin{align*} P(T^+) &= P(T^+ \cap C) + P(T^+ \cap C^c)\\[6pt] &= P(T^+ \mid C)\,P(C) + P(T^+ \mid C^c)\,P(C^c) \end{align*} \]

Plugging these known values directly into the formula:

\[ \begin{align*} P(C \mid T^+) &= \frac{P(T^+ \mid C)\,P(C)}{P(T^+ \mid C)\,P(C) + P(T^+ \mid C^c)\,P(C^c)} \\[6pt] &= \frac{(0.85)(0.01)}{(0.85)(0.01) + (0.05)(0.99)} \\[6pt] &= \frac{0.0085}{0.0085 + 0.0495} \\[6pt] &= \frac{0.0085}{0.0580} \\[6pt] &\approx 0.1466 \end{align*} \]

So the probability of actually having cancer, given a positive test, is about 0.147.

Interpreting the result

Prior to the evidence of a positive test result, the \(P(C)\) was \(0.01\). After receiving a positive test result, our updated belief about having cancer, \(P(C \mid T^+)\), increased to \(0.147\). You might be surprised that the updated probability is not higher given we had a positive test.

Even though the test correctly identifies cancer 85% of the time (sensitivity) and correctly clears cancer-free patients 95% of the time (specificity), a positive result only translates to about a 14.7% chance of actually having cancer.

This feels surprising at first, but the tree diagram makes it clear why: because cancer is rare in the population (only 1% prevalence), the proportion of the total population who receive a false positive (\(0.99 \times 0.05 = 0.0495\)) vastly outweighs the proportion of the total population of people with cancer who correctly test positive (\(0.01 \times 0.85 = 0.0085\)). Most positive tests come from the large healthy population producing a small percentage of false positives, not from the small diseased population testing correctly.
